How an infusion clinic gets paid

01

The operating model, in one view

An infusion visit produces clinical documentation, billable services, a payer claim, and eventually a patient balance. These progress on separate clocks and must be tied back together.

1

Care happens

The clinician documents what was ordered, delivered, observed, and completed in the EMR.

2

Services become charges

Drug, supplies, administration, and related services are converted into code-level charges.

3

The payer decides

A clean claim is sent, processed under the member’s benefits and the clinic’s contract, then paid or denied.

4

Balances are resolved

Payments, contractual adjustments, patient responsibility, follow-up, and accounting records are reconciled.

E

EMR documentation is the clinical source

For an infusion, the record commonly includes the order and diagnosis, drug name and NDC or inventory reference, administered dose and units, route, waste, administration times, nursing documentation, and any required signatures. Billing cannot safely assume a scheduled infusion was actually delivered as planned.

$

The billing system is the financial source

It holds the charge ledger, submitted claims, payer and patient balances, remittance posting, collection notes, adjustments, and reporting. It is where the clinic determines what is still owed and by whom.

03

From treatment record to submitted claim

Early steps aim to make the claim match the care delivered, coverage requirements, and payer formatting rules before the payer ever sees it.

Registration, eligibility, and authorization

Before service, staff validate identity and coverage, determine whether the clinic is in-network, check active benefits, and identify referral or prior-authorization requirements. The authorization must align with the planned drug, dates, place of service, units, and payer rules.

Output: a financially cleared appointment, an exception queue, or an informed patient discussion.

Clinical documentation in the EMR

The treating team confirms the order and records the actual care. Variations matter: a treatment may be held, dose-reduced, wasted, split across days, or associated with a different diagnosis than expected.

Output: a signed, billable treatment record with enough support for the codes.

Charge capture and coding

Charges may flow from the EMR automatically, be entered from an infusion worksheet, or be reviewed by a charge team. The clinic selects or verifies diagnosis codes, drug/supply codes, administration codes, units, modifiers, dates, rendering provider, and place of service.

Output: charge lines that describe what occurred and can be billed under payer rules.

Charge review and claim scrubbing

Rules check for missing demographics, inactive coverage, invalid coding combinations, duplicate charges, unit mismatches, required authorization, timely-filing risk, and payer-specific edits. A failed edit becomes a work item for a biller, coder, clinical team, or registration staff member.

Output: a clean claim or a corrected charge / registration record.

Claim creation, batching, and submission

The billing system groups clean claims for transmission—often daily or several times daily. The electronic healthcare claim is commonly an 837 transaction. A batch can contain many patients and payers, while each claim remains independently traceable.

Output: a transmission record with a submitted date, payer route, and control number.

Transmission acknowledgments and status

The clinic first learns whether the file and claim were accepted for processing, not whether they will be paid. Staff monitor acknowledgments, payer portal status, and claim-status responses. Unacknowledged claims can become a serious “lost claim” risk.

Output: accepted for payer adjudication, rejected for correction, or escalated for follow-up.

!

Code choice is fact-specific. The categories above are deliberately broad. Actual coding depends on the documented treatment, coding guidance, payer contract, coverage policy, and setting of care. A scheduled drug does not by itself establish a billable claim.

04

Clearinghouse versus payer portal

These are complementary, not interchangeable. The clearinghouse is typically the electronic traffic hub; the payer portal is often the payer-specific research and exception workspace.

Clearinghouse

Routes and validates transactions at scale

  • Receives claim batches from the billing system and checks technical format and many front-end rules.
  • Routes each claim to the appropriate payer or network gateway.
  • Returns acknowledgments and may return payer claim-status or remittance files.
  • Lets a billing team monitor rejections and transmission history across many payers.

It does not make the coverage or payment decision.

Payer portal

Exposes the payer’s own view of the claim

  • Shows detailed eligibility, authorization, claim, remit, and appeal information for that payer.
  • May support attachments, corrected claims, reconsiderations, and secure messages.
  • Is frequently used to research a denial or a claim that appears stalled.
  • Can differ widely in data quality and workflow from one payer to another.

It is often the final source for payer-specific detail.

05

Adjudication turns a charge into a payer decision

The payer compares the submitted claim with the member’s coverage, prior authorization, medical policy, benefits accumulated to date, and its contract with the clinic.

Paid as expected

The remittance indicates allowed amount, payer payment, contractual adjustment, and any patient responsibility. The account moves from insurance AR to the remaining patient balance, if any.

Pended or partially paid

Some lines may need records, an authorization update, coordination-of-benefits work, or an underpayment review. The unpaid portion remains in insurance AR or a review queue.

Denied

The payer processed the claim but did not pay a line or claim as submitted. The remittance reason and payer policy determine whether it is corrected, appealed, written off, or billed to a responsible party.

Before adjudication

The claim’s billed charge is on the ledger. The expected collectible amount usually sits in insurance AR, while any pre-service payment is held as a credit or deposit.

Insurance AR is provisional

After adjudication

The payer balance is resolved or worked further. The patient owes only the adjudicated responsibility less any deposit already applied.

06

Patient billing: estimate first, reconcile later

For expensive infusion therapy, clinics often discuss and collect an estimated amount before service. The estimate is useful, but it is not the final payer determination.

A

Pre-service estimate

Financial-clearance or front-desk staff combine plan benefit information, deductible and out-of-pocket status, expected codes/units, the clinic’s expected allowed amount, authorization status, and the clinic’s collection policy.

  • It may produce a copay, a deposit request, a payment plan offer, or a financial-assistance discussion.
  • It can be wrong because other claims may process first, treatment details may change, or the payer may apply a different rule or allowed amount.
  • A payment received at this point should normally remain identifiable as a patient credit until the final responsibility is known.
B

Post-adjudication patient statement

Once the ERA is posted, the system applies the patient deposit, calculates the remaining patient balance, and creates a statement or payment request according to the clinic’s policy.

  • If the deposit was too low, the clinic bills the remaining balance or starts a payment plan.
  • If it was too high, the account carries a credit, applies it to other valid balances, or triggers a refund under policy.
  • Staff must handle disputes, returned mail, installment plans, and collections escalation with documented notes.

Accounts receivable (AR) work queues

Billing teams sort outstanding balances by payer, age, dollar amount, denial reason, treatment type, and next action. A collector’s work often includes reviewing EOB/ERA details, logging into portals, making calls, uploading records, coordinating with clinical staff, filing appeals, and documenting every attempt.

✓

What “resolved” means

A claim is not necessarily resolved when cash arrives. The account needs a supported disposition for every balance: payer payment, contractual adjustment, patient responsibility, valid secondary payment, corrected claim in process, approved write-off, refund, or another documented outcome.

08

Reconciliation keeps records and cash aligned

Reconciliation is a series of control checks—not just a monthly accounting task. It asks whether the clinic’s clinical record, claim ledger, remittance detail, and bank deposits agree.

Daily / ongoing controls

  • Compare infusion schedules and completed administrations to captured charges.
  • Confirm submitted batches received acknowledgments and no rejected claims were left behind.
  • Match ERA payments to EFT trace numbers and deposit dates.
  • Match card settlements, checks, and lockbox files to patient-account postings.
  • Review unapplied cash, credit balances, and duplicate payments.

Periodic financial controls

  • Reconcile billing-system cash totals to the bank and general ledger.
  • Review contractual adjustments and write-offs for correct authorization.
  • Investigate payer underpayments, reversals, recoupments, and offsets.
  • Review refunds and patient credit balances against clinic policy.
  • Validate that AR aging and revenue reports use consistent cut-off dates.
$

ERA and EFT must be paired. An ERA explains how a payer applied a payment; the EFT is the cash movement. The same payment may cover many claims, include offsets or recoupments, or arrive on a different day from the remit file.

09

Reporting turns workflow into management visibility

Useful reports connect operational bottlenecks to financial impact. They are usually segmented by payer, location, service line, treatment type, and responsible work team.

Charge lag

Days from treatment to charge entry. Long lag delays every downstream step.

Clean-claim / first-pass rate

Share of claims accepted and paid without avoidable correction or rework.

Denial rate & reason mix

Denials as a share of claims or dollars, grouped by root cause and payer.

Days in AR

How long outstanding receivables remain open, with aging buckets such as 0–30, 31–60, 61–90, and 90+ days.

Net collection rate

Collected allowed revenue relative to collectible revenue after contract adjustments.

Authorization performance

Scheduled cases with valid, matching authorization versus cases delayed or denied for authorization issues.

Patient estimate variance

Difference between pre-service estimated responsibility and final adjudicated responsibility.

Credit & unapplied cash

Patient or payer money received but not yet correctly applied, refunded, or resolved.

Where a billing vendor’s scope typically begins and ends

Contracts vary. The provider retains the patient relationship, clinical documentation, compliance accountability, and key financial policies—even when a vendor performs much of the operational work.

Basic billing vendor

Usually focused on claims after service

  • May receive finalized charges from the clinic, create claims, submit them, post remittances, and follow up AR.
  • Often relies on the provider’s staff for eligibility, authorization, patient estimates, documentation fixes, and front-desk collection.
  • May flag coding or documentation issues, but clinic personnel often supply the correction.
  • Patient statements and inbound patient calls may be inside or outside scope depending on the agreement.

Full-service RCM vendor

May operate from pre-service through patient balance

  • Can support benefits verification, authorization tracking, financial clearance, estimates, collections, coding, charge review, claims, and AR.
  • May run patient financial communications, payment processing, statements, payment plans, and post-adjudication follow-up.
  • Still depends on timely, accurate clinical documentation and on provider decisions about policy, exceptions, and patient experience.
  • Needs clear handoffs for clinical questions, refunds, write-offs, appeals, and escalation authority.

11

Glossary

A compact vocabulary for reading billing screens, payer files, and workflow reports.

Adjudication
The payer’s processing of a claim to determine coverage, allowed amount, payer payment, adjustments, and patient responsibility.
Allowed amount
The amount recognized under a payer’s contract or plan rules—not necessarily the clinic’s billed charge.
AR (accounts receivable)
Money the clinic expects to collect, separated into insurer AR, patient AR, and sometimes other responsible parties.
Charge capture
Converting documented services, drugs, supplies, and administration into billable charge lines.
Claim scrubber
A rules-based pre-submission check that identifies data, coding, and payer-rule issues.
Contractual adjustment
The difference between a billed charge and the allowed amount that the clinic agrees not to collect under contract; it is not normally patient responsibility.
ERA / 835
Electronic Remittance Advice: the payer’s electronic explanation of claim adjudication and payment.
EFT
Electronic funds transfer: the payer’s actual deposit of money into the clinic’s account.
EOB
Explanation of Benefits: a member-facing summary of how the payer processed a claim; it is not itself a bill.
Prior authorization
Payer approval required before certain services or drugs are covered, generally tied to a defined scope and time period.
Rejection
A claim not accepted into normal processing, usually because it failed a front-end technical or data edit.
Denial
A payer decision after processing to not pay all or part of a claim as submitted.
Timely filing
The deadline by which a payer requires the claim or corrected claim to be received.
Unapplied cash
Money received but not yet correctly matched to the intended patient account, claim, or charge line.
